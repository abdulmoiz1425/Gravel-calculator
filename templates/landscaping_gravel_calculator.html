How Much Landscaping Gravel Do You Need?

For a rectangular area, multiply the length by the width to find the area, then multiply by the planned depth to calculate volume. When length and width are in feet and depth is in inches, divide the depth by 12 before calculating.

  • Cubic feet: length × width × depth in feet
  • Cubic yards: cubic feet ÷ 27
  • Estimated weight: cubic yards × the selected material's approximate weight per cubic yard

Example: A garden bed measuring 18 feet by 6 feet with a 2-inch gravel layer needs approximately 18 cubic feet, or 0.67 cubic yards, before any additional ordering allowance. The estimated weight depends on the selected aggregate's density.

For an L-shaped or irregular area, divide the space into smaller rectangles, calculate each section, and add the volumes together.

For a useful estimate, measure the prepared area and choose a depth suited to the project. Decorative gravel, pedestrian paths, and structural bases may require different materials and installation methods.

How Much Gravel Do I Need for Landscaping?

Length & Width

Measure the full coverage area in feet. For irregular shapes, measure the longest length and widest width, then adjust slightly for missing corners.

Inches

A shallow layer may work for a decorative border, while a path or patio needs a deeper layer. Too little gravel leaves bare spots after rain or foot traffic.

Type of Stone

Material & Density

Different stones have different densities. Fine pea gravel weighs differently than large river rock. Always ask your supplier for the exact tons-per-yard figure.

Tip: The suggested order already includes a 10% allowance for uneven edges, low spots, and settling. Ordering too much costs more, but running short means a second delivery charge.

Choosing the Right Gravel Depth

The appropriate depth depends on the type of landscaping project, the existing ground, and how the area will be used. A decorative garden bed may require a different layer from a walkway or patio area.

Consider whether the ground needs preparation, whether a base layer is required, and how the gravel will be contained. For paths and frequently used surfaces, a stable base and suitable edging can help improve the finished result. Follow project-specific guidance instead of applying one depth to every landscaping job.

Which Gravel Type Is Best for Landscaping?

The best landscaping aggregate depends on the project's appearance, drainage needs, foot traffic, and maintenance requirements.

Gravel TypeBest ForBenefitsWatch Out For
Pea gravel Paths, patios, play areas, garden beds Smooth, rounded, and comfortable underfoot Can move around without edging
Crushed gravel Paths, base layers, drainage Locks together better than rounded stone May feel rough when walked on barefoot
River rock Decorative beds and dry creek areas Natural look and larger stone options Usually costs more and is less stable for paths

Pea gravel: Small, rounded stones often used for decorative beds and selected paths. Because the stones can move, edging can help keep them contained.

River rock: Rounded stones available in different sizes and colors. Larger pieces are often used for decorative features and some landscaping drainage applications.

Decomposed granite: A finer material often selected for paths and surfaces where a compacted finish is desired. Performance depends on the product and installation.

Crushed stone: Angular particles that may interlock more effectively than rounded stones, depending on the grade and how the material is installed.

Material names and specifications vary by supplier. Check the stone size, grading, color, and intended application before ordering. For drainage or load-bearing uses, confirm that the chosen product is suitable for the job.

Cubic Yards vs. Tons: What Should You Order?

Volume and weight are not the same — confirm which unit your supplier uses before ordering

Landscaping suppliers may quote gravel by cubic yard, ton, bag, or truckload. A cubic yard measures volume. A ton measures weight. The same cubic yard of gravel can weigh different amounts depending on stone size, density, and moisture. Fine crushed stone may weigh differently from large river rock.

How to Measure an Irregular Landscaping Area

Not every landscape project is a perfect rectangle — curved beds and winding paths need a simple estimating approach

For Curved & Irregular Areas

  1. Measure the longest length of the curved area.
  2. Measure the widest width.
  3. Multiply those measurements to get a rough coverage area.
  4. Reduce the result slightly if large corners are missing.
  5. Use the suggested order (base + 10%) for curves, borders, and uneven ground.

For Winding Paths

Divide the path into smaller rectangular sections. Calculate each section separately, then add the totals together. This is more accurate than guessing one large area.

Circular Areas: Use the Circle tab in the calculator above. Measure the diameter from one side to the other and enter it as the diameter — the calculator handles the rest.

Common Landscaping Gravel Ordering Mistakes

A good estimate prevents wasted money and an extra delivery charge

Measuring Before Ground Preparation

  • Grass, old mulch, and soil removal can change the final depth.
  • Measure after preparing the area whenever possible.
  • A depth measurement taken too early leads to under-ordering.

Forgetting to Convert Inches to Feet

  • Most landscaping depths are in inches, but formulas use feet.
  • Divide depth in inches by 12 before calculating.
  • 2 inches = 0.167 ft — this single step changes everything.

Ordering the Exact Minimum

  • Gravel does not spread perfectly across every area.
  • Use the suggested order (base + 10%) for grading, curves, and low spots instead of guessing a percentage.
  • A second delivery costs more than a little extra material upfront.

Ignoring Edging

  • Pea gravel and small decorative stone shift without edging.
  • Stones spread into grass, mulch, or walkways over time.
  • Plan and budget for edging before ordering material.

Using One Gravel Type for Every Job

  • Rounded stone looks good in beds but shifts under foot traffic.
  • Crushed gravel provides more stability for paths and base layers.
  • Match the material to the project, not just the look.

Forgetting Delivery Access

  • Check whether the delivery truck can reach your drop location.
  • Ask your supplier where material will be unloaded.
  • Confirm how much space the truck needs before scheduling.
